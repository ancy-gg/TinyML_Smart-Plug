\section{Socket-Heating Protection Validation}
\label{sec:ch4-socket-heating-protection}

Socket-heating protection validation evaluated the relay-facing estimated
socket temperature under the same general load progression used during
characterization. This validation was necessary because the socket-heating
function does not trip from the raw thermistor value alone; instead, the
firmware uses the compensated estimated socket temperature described in
\textit{Section~\ref{sec:ch3-socket-heating-models}} and evaluated by the
protection logic in \textit{Section~\ref{sec:ch3-protection-logic}}. The
methodology compared the device temperature measurement, infrared thermometer
reading, estimated socket temperature, and expected normal socket temperature:
the thermistor channel provided the device temperature, the infrared thermometer
served as the contact-region reference, the estimated socket temperature served
as the protection-facing value, and the expected normal socket temperature
served as the estimator's internal comparator.

This distinction is important because poor plug-socket contact can create
localized heating even when total current does not necessarily force
conventional protection to operate [17], [24], [46], [47]. It is also consistent
with the reviewed literature showing that an external temperature sensor can lag
or under-read the true internal hotspot because the hottest point is usually
inside the contact interface [27]-[31]. For this reason, the trip threshold was
applied to the estimated socket temperature, while the infrared thermometer
reading was used to interpret the contact-region temperature during the same
trial. The plotted trends in this section were generated from the full
protection validation records in \textit{Appendix~\ref{app:d}}.

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-protect-two-lamps}} shows that higher current
increased socket heating for both socket conditions, but the corroded socket
tripped earlier because the degraded contact produced additional localized
heating beyond the current effect. This supports the interpretation that contact
condition influenced the trip time, not current magnitude alone.

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-protect-three-lamps}} shows fast thermal
development in the corroded case. At this load level, the degraded socket
reached a hazardous contact-region temperature within a short observation
window, with the reference terminal temperature reaching 55.0{\degreeC}. This
result shows that the poor contact condition accelerated the thermal rise beyond
what would be expected from current level alone. It also supports the use of the
estimated socket temperature as a conservative relay-facing value, since the
external measurement path may still represent a lower and delayed view of the
actual contact-region heating [27]-[31].

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-protect-four-lamps}} shows the most severe heating
case in the protection validation set. The corroded socket tripped after 5min
when the estimated socket temperature reached 40.0{\degreeC}, while the
reference terminal temperature had already reached 56.0{\degreeC}. Compared
with the lower-current trials, this case shows that high current increased the
thermal stress, but the degraded contact made the heating rise much faster. This
is consistent with the reviewed literature showing that poor plug-socket contact
can create localized heating that is not fully represented by external
temperature measurement or current magnitude alone [17], [24], [27]-[31],
[46], [47].

\textit{Table~\ref{tab:ch4-heating-protection}} summarizes how the relay
response changed with socket condition and load level. At low current, the
corroded socket did not trip because the estimated socket temperature remained
below the 40{\degreeC} threshold. As the load current increased, the corroded
socket tripped earlier than the normal socket because degraded contact added
localized heating at the plug-socket interface. This is consistent with the
reviewed literature showing that poor contact pressure, oxidation, and degraded
contact condition can increase local thermal stress without requiring a
classical overcurrent event [17], [24], [46], [47].

The table also shows why the relay-facing estimate must be interpreted as an
indirect protection value. In the severe degraded-contact cases, the reference
terminal temperature was higher than both the device temperature measurement and
the estimated socket temperature. This follows the reviewed literature showing
that external temperature sensing can under-read a hidden contact hotspot due to
sensor placement, thermal separation, and response delay [27]-[31]. Thus, the
40{\degreeC} estimated-temperature threshold served as a conservative
socket-heating trip point for the prototype, not as the actual internal contact
temperature.

These results support the study's objective of detecting contact heating at the
plug-socket interface through complementary socket-level protection. The relay
action was not based on RMS current alone, and the results show that similar
current levels can lead to different thermal behavior when the socket condition
changes. This confirms the need for local thermal supervision, since localized
contact heating may develop before conventional current-based protection can
directly observe the fault.
