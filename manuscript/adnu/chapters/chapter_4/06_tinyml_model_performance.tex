\section{TinyML Model Performance}
\label{sec:ch4-tinyml-model-performance}

The TinyML results were interpreted at two levels. First, the embedded
prototype was evaluated through trial-level relay behavior in the laboratory.
Second, the exported Random Forest report was evaluated through held-out
frame-level and event-level metrics. This two-level interpretation is necessary
because the relay decision represents the complete protection sequence, while
the frame-level metrics show the behavior of the model before persistence
counting and relay actuation are applied. This follows the methodology in
\textit{Section~\ref{sec:ch3-feature-contract}}, where the deployed arc model
was defined as a feature-based TinyML classifier whose output is further
processed by firmware protection logic.

This distinction is important because series arc-fault detection in the
prototype was not based on a single instantaneous model output. The Random
Forest classifier identified arc-like waveform frames from the selected
features, but the relay response still depended on the protection manager
confirming repeated or persistent evidence before actuation. This arrangement
connects the TinyML model to the study objective of socket-level complementary
protection: the model provides compact embedded inference for waveform
disturbance, while the firmware logic reduces the chance that isolated
transient behavior will immediately become a relay trip. This is consistent
with the reviewed literature showing that series arcs can be intermittent,
load-dependent, and difficult to separate from normal nonlinear-load
distortion using RMS current alone [33]-[39], [49]-[54].

\textit{Table~\ref{tab:ch4-trial-metrics}} and
\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-group-metrics}} show high overall performance
across 200 trial outcomes. Precision is related to nuisance-trip reduction:
the 98.97\% overall precision means that only one normal event was incorrectly
tripped in the trial set. Recall is related to safety sensitivity: the 96.00\%
overall recall means that four arc trials were missed. The recognized-load
group had slightly higher recall and no normal-event false trip, while the
unrecognized-load group accounted for three of the four missed arc trials and
the single false trip. This is consistent with the reviewed literature showing
that arc detection is load-dependent and that normal nonlinear loads can
produce waveform behavior that complicates fault interpretation [8], [33],
[40]-[42].

The trial-level classifier and persistence logic therefore produced balanced
performance for the tested conditions. Precision remained high for
nuisance-trip reduction, while recall remained high enough to detect most
induced arc trials. These results apply to the deployed feature contract
described in \textit{Section~\ref{sec:ch3-feature-contract}}: six selected
waveform features were used by the arc model, with context fields appended to
the model vector. This supports the design choice of treating series arc-fault
detection as a multi-feature and context-aware inference problem rather than as
a direct RMS-current threshold decision [33]-[39], [49]-[54].

\textit{Table~\ref{tab:ch4-per-load-metrics}} identifies the load cases that
contributed to the remaining trial-level errors. The impact drill is the only
load listed with precision below 100\%, meaning it contributed the
normal-event false trip. The handheld vacuum, Electrolux vacuum, angle
grinder, and bench cutter had lower recall, meaning those load cases
contributed missed arc trials. These results show that the residual errors
were concentrated in brushed or universal-motor loads rather than distributed
equally across all tested devices.

load; and the handheld vacuum, Electrolux vacuum, angle grinder, bench cutter,
and impact drill were treated as brushed or universal-motor loads. These
aggregations summarize the recorded trial tables in

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-family-outcomes}} is normalized because the
family groups did not contain the same number of devices or trials. In
\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-family-outcomes}(a)}, resistive and inductive
load-family trials had 100\% arc detection, while the brushed-load family had
92\% arc detection and 8\% missed arc trials. In
\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-family-outcomes}(b)}, the brushed-load family
also contained the only false trip, giving 98\% correct normal classification
and 2\% false-trip outcome for that family. The normalized view therefore
shows that the brushed or universal-motor group was the main source of
residual model error rather than simply the largest group in raw counts. This
supports the reviewed finding that motor-type and nonlinear loads can produce
commutation effects, waveform irregularity, and harmonic behavior that require
context-aware interpretation [8], [40]-[42].

The exported Random Forest report provides a second view of performance. The
held-out test split contained 10,268 feature rows, including 90 positive rows.
The frame-level confusion matrix contained 10,173 true negatives, 5 false
positives, 25 false negatives, and 65 true positives. The corresponding
frame-level accuracy was 99.7078\%, precision was 92.8571\%, recall was
72.2222\%, and F1-score was 81.25\%. At event level, the same report showed
25 true arc events, 21 detected events, 4 missed events, 31 predicted events,
1 false-alarm event, 84.0\% event recall, 96.7742\% event precision, and
89.9358\% event F1-score. The data-preparation, training-report, and
exported-model details supporting these metrics are summarized in
\textit{Appendix~\ref{app:f-tinyml-training-summary}}.

The model-report values are lower than the final trial-level relay metrics
because they evaluate individual frames and grouped arc events before the full
firmware protection sequence is interpreted at trial level. This difference
clarifies the role of the persistence counter: isolated model outputs are not
treated as final relay decisions until they satisfy the firmware's protection
logic. The deployed arc-detection result should therefore be interpreted as a
model-plus-firmware protection behavior, where the Random Forest identifies
arc-like frames and the persistence logic converts repeated evidence into a
relay action.

\section{Model Limitations}
\label{sec:ch4-model-limitations}

The main limitation is dataset coverage. The tests included recognized and
unrecognized loads, but they did not cover all appliance families, wiring
conditions, electrode materials, contact gaps, ambient conditions, or utility
waveform variations. This limitation is consistent with the reviewed arc-fault
literature, which identifies load dependence, limited data coverage, false
alarms, and missed detections as recurring challenges in AI-based arc-fault
detection [33]. The results therefore support controlled prototype feasibility
within the tested scope, but they do not establish AFCI standard compliance or
replacement equivalence with certified protective devices.

The limitation is also important for interpreting the unrecognized-load and
brushed-load results. The prototype generalized to most tested cases, but the
remaining missed arcs and the single false trip show that wider load-family
coverage would be required before the model could be treated as broadly
representative of residential appliance behavior. This follows the reviewed
literature showing that healthy nonlinear loads can produce waveform
disturbance that overlaps with arc-related indicators, making load context an
important part of false-positive control [8], [40]-[42].
