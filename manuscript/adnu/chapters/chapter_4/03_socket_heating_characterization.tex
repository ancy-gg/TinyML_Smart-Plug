\section{Socket-Heating Characterization}
\label{sec:ch4-socket-heating-characterization}

The socket-heating characterization compared a normal socket with a degraded
socket under similar current levels. This section presents only the flat NTC
temperature measurements obtained after resistance-to-temperature conversion
using the Steinhart-Hart/Beta equation described in
\textit{Section~\ref{sec:ch3-device-temperature-calibration}}; the thermal
constants, expected normal socket temperature, and estimated socket temperature
were not yet applied to the plotted data.

The characterization was performed to verify that socket heating is affected not
only by current, but also by contact condition. This follows the reviewed
literature showing that poor plug-socket contact can produce localized heating
while the current path remains functional [17], [24], [46], [47], and that an
external temperature measurement may lag or under-read the internal hotspot
because the hottest region is usually inside the contact interface [27]-[31].
The graphs presented in this section are derived from the characterization
records in \textit{Appendix~\ref{app:c}}.

The 38.2{\degreeC} comparison point was used only as a common reference marker
for comparing how quickly each socket condition approached the same measured
thermistor temperature level.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \socketCharPlot{1}{240}
  \caption{Water heater temperature trend during socket-heating characterization.}
  \label{fig:ch4-char-water-heater}
\end{figure}

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-char-water-heater}} shows that at about 5.2A, both
socket conditions stayed below the 38.2{\degreeC} comparison point within the
240-minute window. However, the corroded socket still ended hotter than the
normal socket in both the device temperature measurement and the infrared
terminal reading. This indicates that the contact-condition effect was already
present at the lowest tested load.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \socketCharPlot{2}{240}
  \caption{Water heater plus one halogen lamp temperature trend during characterization.}
  \label{fig:ch4-char-one-lamp}
\end{figure}

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-char-one-lamp}} shows clearer separation between
normal and corroded behavior. The corroded socket reached the 38.2{\degreeC}
comparison point, while the normal socket did not reach that point within the
same window.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \socketCharPlot{3}{240}
  \caption{Water heater plus two halogen lamps temperature trend during characterization.}
  \label{fig:ch4-char-two-lamps}
\end{figure}

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-char-two-lamps}} shows that the degraded socket
reached the comparison temperature much earlier than the normal socket. The
infrared terminal temperature also rose above the device
measurement, especially in the corroded case. This supports treating 
the external thermistor as an indirect reading rather
than the actual hotspot temperature [27]-[31].

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \socketCharPlot{4}{240}
  \caption{Water heater plus three halogen lamps temperature trend during characterization.}
  \label{fig:ch4-char-three-lamps}
\end{figure}

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-char-three-lamps}} shows the strongest
time-to-threshold contrast in the characterization set. The corroded socket
reached the comparison point after 7 minutes, while the normal socket reached it
after 52 minutes. Since the current levels were similar, this difference is
attributed mainly to degraded contact condition, supporting the reviewed finding
that poor contact can accelerate localized heating [17], [24], [46], [47].

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \socketCharPlot{5}{195}
  \caption{Water heater plus four halogen lamps temperature trend during characterization.}
  \label{fig:ch4-char-four-lamps}
\end{figure}

\textit{Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-char-four-lamps}} shows that the high-current
degraded trial developed rapidly and was stopped after a short observation
window. The terminal temperature reached 45.8{\degreeC} within 4 minutes even
though the final device temperature measurement was only 36.9{\degreeC}. This
difference shows that the NTC thermistor, as placed in the prototype, could
under-read the contact-region temperature during severe degraded-contact
heating.

\textit{Table~\ref{tab:ch4-heating-characterization}} confirms that degraded
contact changed both heating speed and hotspot severity. At about 9A, the
corroded socket reached 38.2{\degreeC} in 48 minutes compared with 105 minutes
for the normal socket. At about 11A, the corroded socket reached the same point
in 7 minutes compared with 52 minutes for the normal socket. These differences
show that similar current levels did not produce identical thermal behavior when
the socket condition changed. This supports the reviewed finding that poor
contact condition increases localized heating risk through contact resistance
and thermal concentration [17], [24], [46], [47].

The table also shows why the compensated thermal model was needed after
characterization. In the higher-temperature and degraded-contact cases, the
infrared terminal reading was consistently higher than the device temperature
measurement. For example, in the water heater plus three-lamp corroded case, the
device temperature measurement was 38.8{\degreeC}, while the infrared terminal
reading reached 53.0{\degreeC}. In the water heater plus four-lamp corroded
case, the device temperature measurement was 36.9{\degreeC}, while the infrared
terminal reading reached 45.8{\degreeC}. These observations demonstrate that
the flat NTC thermistor measurement alone did not fully represent the hotter
contact region. This is consistent with the reviewed literature explaining that
external temperature sensing can under-read an internal hotspot because of
thermal separation, sensor placement, and response delay [27]-[31].

These characterization findings verify why socket-level thermal monitoring was included in the
prototype. The flat NTC measurements were able to show the heating trend of each
socket, but they did not fully represent the hotter contact-region temperature.
This supports the use of a compensated protection-facing temperature estimate,
since localized socket heating may develop in a region that conventional
current-based protection and external temperature measurement do not directly
observe.
