\section{Series Arc-Fault Detection}
\label{sec:ch4-series-arc-detection}

The series arc-fault tests evaluated whether waveform disturbance and conduction
intermittence could be converted into a relay decision. During arcing, the
current waveform was expected to show low-current gaps, restrike spikes,
unstable cycle shape, and changes in mid/high-frequency content. This follows
the reviewed literature showing that series arcs may remain below conventional
breaker trip levels while still producing waveform abnormalities that require
multi-feature interpretation rather than RMS-current magnitude alone [16],
[33]-[39], [49]-[54]. These behaviors correspond to the features defined in
\textit{Section~\ref{sec:ch3-waveform-features}}, especially
\texttt{max\_low\_current\_run\_ms}, \texttt{low\_current\_ratio},
\texttt{zero\_dwell\_ratio}, \texttt{midband\_residual\_ratio}, and
\texttt{spectral\_flux\_midhf}.

The trial-level results are summarized as confusion matrices in
\textit{Table~\ref{tab:ch4-arc-confusion}}.

\textit{Table~\ref{tab:ch4-arc-confusion}} shows that the prototype detected
96 of 100 arc trials and produced one relay trip during 100 normal trials. The
recognized-load group had no normal-event false trip and one missed arc event,
while the unrecognized-load group had three missed arc events and one false
trip. This difference is consistent with the reviewed literature showing that
healthy nonlinear loads can produce waveform distortion, conduction gaps, or
spectral behavior that may resemble fault behavior when load context is not
fully represented [8], [33], [40]-[42]. Thus, the unrecognized-load results
show the practical importance of context-aware inference in reducing nuisance
operation while still preserving sensitivity to arc-related waveform changes.

The result supports the arc-detection objective under the tested laboratory
conditions. The classifier response was strongest when the arc created
persistent low-current gaps and spectral disturbance, allowing the leaky arc
counter to reach the relay-trip threshold. The missed trials indicate that not
all arcs produced equally strong or persistent feature responses, especially
when the load type differed from the fitted set. This interpretation is
consistent with the reviewed finding that series-arc detection is stochastic
and load-dependent, and that reliable detection depends on combining several
time-domain and frequency-domain indicators rather than treating one feature as
conclusive evidence [33]-[39], [49]-[54]. Therefore, the prototype is best
interpreted as a controlled feasibility demonstration of socket-level
complementary arc-fault protection, not as a certified AFCI-equivalent device.

\section{Arc-Fault Dataset Summary}
\label{sec:ch4-arc-dataset-summary}

The cleaned arc-training dataset contained 49,296 rows, of which 283 were arc
markers, equivalent to about 0.577\% of the retained data. This confirms that
the arc fault dataset was highly imbalanced: most frames represented normal operation
even in sessions that contained an arc event. The imbalance explains why
frame-level accuracy alone can be misleading, since a classifier can appear
accurate by predicting mostly normal frames. Precision, recall, F1-score,
confusion matrices, and event-level metrics were therefore used to interpret
the safety behavior of the prototype. This follows the methodology in
\textit{Section~\ref{sec:ch3-tinyml-workflow}}, where the arc model was
evaluated not only by row-level classification but also by its ability to
support relay decisions during grouped arc episodes.

The detailed trial records are presented in \textit{Appendices~\ref{app:e}
and~\ref{app:f}}. These records extend the summarized results by showing the
arc markers, feature-frame behavior, and relay-state transitions used to verify
the trial outcomes.
