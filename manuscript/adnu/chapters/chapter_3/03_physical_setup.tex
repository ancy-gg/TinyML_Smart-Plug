\section{Physical Setup and Hardware Implementation}
\label{sec:ch3-physical-setup}

The physical setup translates the system model into the actual prototype used
for testing. This section introduces the hardware blocks, full schematic,
signal-conditioning path, enclosure arrangement, and component roles before
the calibration and protection computations are presented.

\subsection{Sensing Subsystems and Hardware Blocks}
\label{sec:ch3-sensing-subsystems}

The physical implementation combined a line-side plug, protected outlet,
solid-state relay module, ESP32-S3 controller, voltage sensor, current sensor,
NTC thermistor, anti-aliasing circuit, backup supply, OLED display, buzzer,
and enclosure. The hardware was organized so that mains conductors, sensing
circuits, low-voltage logic, and user-interface wiring could be traced
separately during testing.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-block-diagram} gives the hardware block-level view before
the detailed schematic is introduced.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-block-diagram} shows that the relay is positioned as the

\subsection{Full Schematic Discussion}
\label{sec:ch3-full-schematic-discussion}

The full circuit schematic is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-full-schematic}.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[angle=90,width=1.44\textheight,height=1.36\textwidth,keepaspectratio,trim=0.15in 0.50in 0.15in 0.20in,clip]{ch3/full_schematic.pdf}
  \caption{Full schematic of the TinyML Smart Plug prototype.}
  \label{fig:ch3-full-schematic}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-full-schematic} presents the complete hardware
implementation of the TinyML smart plug prototype. The schematic is discussed
according to the circuit flow: AC input protection, low-voltage power
conversion, voltage, current, and temperature sensing, signal conditioning,
analog-to-digital conversion, ESP32-S3 processing, relay fault latching, and
user notification. Appendix B provides the detailed board-layout views, while
this section explains how each block contributes to sensing, protection, and
prototype operation.

The AC input enters through the line-side plug and is routed first to the
input-protection stage. A fuse interrupts abnormal overcurrent conditions,
while a varistor suppresses transient overvoltage events on the line. After
this stage, the live and neutral conductors continue toward the relay-controlled
outlet and the internal power-supply circuit. This places the first hardware
protection layer before the load and before the low-voltage electronics.

The power-supply section converts the mains input into the regulated rails used
by the controller and sensing circuits. These rails supply the ESP32-S3, ADC,
operational amplifiers, sensors, OLED display, buzzer, and relay-control logic.
Diode-isolated routing and local regulation help provide stable low-voltage
power. The rocker-switch path also uses MOSFET switching to reduce the exposure
of the regulated control rail to mechanical switch sparks, contact bounce, and
switching transients during manual switching.

The voltage-sensing path uses the ZMPT101B voltage sensor to provide a scaled
representation of the AC line. This signal is used by the firmware for
RMS-voltage monitoring and mains-present detection. Through this path, the
ESP32-S3 can determine whether the outlet is energized, whether the mains has
vanished, and whether voltage has returned after an interruption, supporting
relay-state handling, fault recovery, and data interpretation.

The current-sensing path uses the ACS37035 current sensor placed in series with
the load. Its analog waveform is the main input for RMS-current computation,
overload monitoring, waveform-feature extraction, and arc-related analysis. The
same sensor also provides a hardware short-circuit output. In this prototype,
that output was configured at $28.28\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{pk}}$, equivalent to
$20\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{RMS}}$ for a sinusoidal waveform. This pin was included
as a backup relay-trip layer independent of the firmware decision, although it
was not formally tested during validation.

The measured current waveform is routed to the anti-aliasing and
signal-conditioning stage before digitization. This stage limits unwanted
high-frequency components and prepares the waveform level for the ADC input.
The conditioned signals are digitized by the MCP3204 analog-to-digital
converter and transferred to the ESP32-S3 through the SPI interface lines
\texttt{SDI-MOSI}, \texttt{SDO-MISO}, \texttt{SPI\_CLK}, and \texttt{CS\_ADC}.
This bridges the analog sensing circuits and the digital protection firmware.

The ESP32-S3 XIAO is the central processing section of the prototype. It
receives digitized waveform data, evaluates voltage and current conditions,
reads the temperature input, executes the local protection logic, controls the
relay command, drives the OLED display, activates the buzzer, and handles
wireless monitoring and logging. The thermistor is installed near the plug-side
wire-contact region to observe localized socket heating. During installation,
the sensing point was insulated with Kapton tape, coupled using thermal paste,
secured with another Kapton layer, and covered with electrical tape to reduce
ambient-air influence on the reading.

The relay circuit acts as the output actuator that connects or disconnects the
protected socket from the input line. It is not driven directly as a simple
ESP32-S3 output. Instead, the relay command passes through a hardware
fault-latching circuit so that a detected fault remains stored after the relay
opens. This is necessary because opening the relay can remove the measured
fault from the sensor inputs. Without a latch, the device could repeatedly
close the relay, detect the same fault, and reopen the relay.

The relay-latching circuit uses two sections of the 74HC74 D flip-flop and an
AND-gate stage. One latch stores the ESP32-side relay or fault command, while
the other stores the short-circuit command from the current-sensor side. The
relay is allowed to close only when both paths indicate that no fault is
present. Thus, the AND-gate stage implements the De Morgan equivalent of a
NOR-style fault decision: the relay remains enabled only when there is no
firmware-side fault and no current-sensor short-circuit fault.

The notification section uses a transistor-driven buzzer controlled by the
ESP32-S3. The buzzer was driven using PWM so that different duty cycles and
pulse patterns could indicate different alert states instead of relying on a
single fixed warning tone. Several delicate low-voltage nodes also include
BAT54S clamp-diode protection to limit abnormal voltage excursions before they
reach the microcontroller, ADC, or sensing interfaces.

The schematic and Appendix B board-layout figures should be interpreted as
prototype-development documents for bench validation. They do not by themselves
certify creepage distance, clearance distance, enclosure safety, thermal safety,
regulatory compliance, or suitability as a replacement for listed protective
devices.

\subsection{Signal Conditioning and Enclosure Implementation}
\label{sec:ch3-signal-conditioning-enclosure}

The anti-aliasing circuit and its frequency response are shown in
Figures~\ref{fig:ch3-aaf-circuit} and~\ref{fig:ch3-aaf-bode}.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{ch3/figure_3.6.png}
  \caption{Second-order multiple-feedback low-pass anti-aliasing circuit.}
  \label{fig:ch3-aaf-circuit}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{ch3/figure_3.7.png}
  \caption{Bode response of the anti-aliasing filter.}
  \label{fig:ch3-aaf-bode}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-aaf-circuit} shows the analog low-pass filter placed
between the current sensor and the ADC. Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-aaf-bode} verifies
that the filter attenuates higher-frequency content before sampling. The
implemented firmware also applies a digital soft anti-aliasing filter in the
current-feature path, so the analog and digital filters work together rather
than as isolated design elements [57].

The frequency-setting values shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-aaf-circuit} were
checked against the standard second-order low-pass relationship
\begin{equation}
  f_0 =
  \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{R_1R_2C_1C_2}} .
  \label{eq:ch3-aaf-cutoff}
\end{equation}
Using the schematic values $R_1=200\mathrm{k}\Omega$,
$R_2=33\mathrm{k}\Omega$, $C_1=470\mathrm{pF}$, and
$C_2=82\mathrm{pF}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  f_0 =
  \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(200\mathrm{k}\Omega)(33\mathrm{k}\Omega)
  (470\mathrm{pF})(82\mathrm{pF})}}
  \approx 9.98\mathrm{kHz}.
  \label{eq:ch3-aaf-cutoff-result}
\end{equation}
This agrees with the plotted cutoff region in Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-aaf-bode},
where the simulated response is approximately -3dB near 10kHz. Since a
second-order Butterworth response has $Q\approx0.707$ and reaches the -3dB
point at its natural frequency, the plotted response was treated as a
near-Butterworth anti-aliasing stage rather than a high-Q peaking filter. The
firmware's additional digital filter uses a similar low-peaking setting
($Q=0.73$) before feature extraction.

The op amp for this stage must support single-supply operation at the
available logic rail, input and output swing around the mid-supply bias,
unity-gain-stable active-filter operation, and enough bandwidth and slew-rate
margin for a 10kHz cutoff [57]. For a conservative 3.3V peak-to-peak signal,
the minimum sinusoidal slew rate is
\begin{equation}
  SR_{\min}=2\pi fV_p
  =2\pi(10\mathrm{kHz})(1.65\mathrm{V})
  \approx 0.104\mathrm{V/\mu s}.
  \label{eq:ch3-aaf-slew}
\end{equation}
The MCP6024-class device indicated in the schematic satisfies this application
class because it is a rail-to-rail input/output, unity-gain-stable, 10MHz
single-supply op amp family [76]. These checks support waveform acquisition
for the prototype tests, but they do not replace full analog compliance
testing of the assembled board.
